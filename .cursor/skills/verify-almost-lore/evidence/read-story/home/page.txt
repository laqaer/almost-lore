Public domain. 1741–1742 The island that kept the captain In November 1741 Vitus Bering’s ship wrecked on an uninhabited island off Kamchatka. He died there on 8 December. Forty-six of the crew got a smaller boat home the next summer, and with them the news that the American coast was not a rumor. 5 min read The Nineteenth Century 7 stories Loans, borders, and empires at their confident peak. Public domain. 1837–1842 The Caroline at Schlosser On the night of 29 December 1837 a British party burned the American steamer Caroline at a landing on the New York side of the Niagara. Amos Durfee was killed on the dock. The near-war was not a second battle at the river. It was a murder charge, a boast, and a sentence about necessity that the two governments adopted without agreeing the raid had earned it. 13 min read Map of the disputed territory, 1839 (engraved by William James Stone), Digital Commonwealth. Public domain. 1839–1842 A line convenient to both In February 1839 the lieutenant-governor of New Brunswick told Maine to pull an armed posse off the Aroostook or he would occupy the disputed territory. On 3 March Congress appropriated ten million dollars and, if invasion was imminent, a ceiling of fifty thousand volunteers. The papers of that winter contain an arrest, a parole, and a threat. They do not contain a battle. The line signed in August 1842 was a conventional boundary, not a finding that the highlands of 1783 had been located. 19 min read Photograph, 1872. Public domain. 1859–1872 The war named for a pig In June 1859 an American settler shot a Hudson’s Bay Company boar on an island the Oregon Treaty had failed to assign. Troops landed. The commemorative line says the pig was the only casualty. The record is a thirteen-year occupation and a boundary drawn in 1872. 6 min read Engraving of the USS San Jacinto stopping the Trent, from Edward Sylvester Ellis (1887). Public domain. 1861–1862 The four men taken off the Trent On 8 November 1861 a Union captain stopped the British mail steamer Trent in the Old Bahama Channel and took off two Confederate commissioners and their secretaries. No one was killed. The near-war was the month Britain spent demanding them back, and the note that gave them back without settling the law. 10 min read G. Philip, London, 1897; Bibliotheque nationale de France. Public domain. 1895–1899 Every means in its power In December 1895 Grover Cleveland told Congress the United States would resist, by every means in its power, a British hold on any land an American commission decided belonged to Venezuela. Britain had just refused to arbitrate the whole boundary. No troops met. The near-miss ended in a treaty, a fifty-year prescription rule, and a line Venezuela later refused to treat as the last word. 11 min read E. Lauro, Cairo, 1898. Public domain. 1898–1899 Two flags at Fashoda In 1898 a French column and an Anglo-Egyptian flotilla met at a ruined fort on the White Nile. Neither side fired. Europe spent the autumn deciding whether that counted as a war. 7 min read 1900 Onward 3 stories The modern record, including the experiments nobody announced. Valentine Series postcard, 1904. Public domain. 1904–1905 Searchlights on the Dogger Bank On the night of 21–22 October 1904 a Russian squadron on its way to the war with Japan fired on Hull trawlers over the Dogger Bank. A commission of inquiry counted two fishermen dead and one trawler sunk, and a majority found no torpedo boats there to fire at. Britain and Russia stayed at peace. The report’s own courtesies are why the outrage and the file are not the same story. 10 min read USS Greenlet at Hunters Point, San Francisco, May 1950; contextual period photograph, not the operation vessel. U.S. Navy. Public domain. September 1950 The fog that was not weather In late September 1950 a Navy vessel sprayed bacteria over San Francisco from offshore. The city was not told. The Army later said the organisms were harmless.
