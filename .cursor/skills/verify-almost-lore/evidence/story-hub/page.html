  • The isthmus that was supposed to pay Scotland

    In July 1698 five Scottish ships carried about twelve hundred people to Darien and called the place Caledonia. They abandoned it in 1699. A second fleet, already at sea, found the fort empty, refounded it, and surrendered to Spain in the spring of 1700.

  • The island that kept the captain

    In November 1741 Vitus Bering’s ship wrecked on an uninhabited island off Kamchatka. He died there on 8 December. Forty-six of the crew got a smaller boat home the next summer, and with them the news that the American coast was not a rumor.

  • The subway that ran one block

    Alfred Ely Beach’s 1870 pneumatic tube under Broadway was a working passenger demo, a political long shot, and a civic almost. New York’s real subway opened thirty-four years later.

    1869–18735 min read

  • The Caroline at Schlosser

    On the night of 29 December 1837 a British party burned the American steamer Caroline at a landing on the New York side of the Niagara. Amos Durfee was killed on the dock. The near-war was not a second battle at the river. It was a murder charge, a boast, and a sentence about necessity that the two governments adopted without agreeing the raid had earned it.

    1837–184213 min read

  • A line convenient to both

    In February 1839 the lieutenant-governor of New Brunswick told Maine to pull an armed posse off the Aroostook or he would occupy the disputed territory. On 3 March Congress appropriated ten million dollars and, if invasion was imminent, a ceiling of fifty thousand volunteers. The papers of that winter contain an arrest, a parole, and a threat. They do not contain a battle. The line signed in August 1842 was a conventional boundary, not a finding that the highlands of 1783 had been located.

    1839–184219 min read

  • The war named for a pig

    In June 1859 an American settler shot a Hudson’s Bay Company boar on an island the Oregon Treaty had failed to assign. Troops landed. The commemorative line says the pig was the only casualty. The record is a thirteen-year occupation and a boundary drawn in 1872.

    1859–18726 min read

  • The four men taken off the Trent

    On 8 November 1861 a Union captain stopped the British mail steamer Trent in the Old Bahama Channel and took off two Confederate commissioners and their secretaries. No one was killed. The near-war was the month Britain spent demanding them back, and the note that gave them back without settling the law.

    1861–186210 min read

  • Every means in its power

    In December 1895 Grover Cleveland told Congress the United States would resist, by every means in its power, a British hold on any land an American commission decided belonged to Venezuela. Britain had just refused to arbitrate the whole boundary. No troops met. The near-miss ended in a treaty, a fifty-year prescription rule, and a line Venezuela later refused to treat as the last word.

    1895–189911 min read

  • Two flags at Fashoda

    In 1898 a French column and an Anglo-Egyptian flotilla met at a ruined fort on the White Nile. Neither side fired. Europe spent the autumn deciding whether that counted as a war.

  • Searchlights on the Dogger Bank

    On the night of 21–22 October 1904 a Russian squadron on its way to the war with Japan fired on Hull trawlers over the Dogger Bank. A commission of inquiry counted two fishermen dead and one trawler sunk, and a majority found no torpedo boats there to fire at. Britain and Russia stayed at peace. The report’s own courtesies are why the outrage and the file are not the same story.

    1904–190510 min read

  • The fog that was not weather

    In late September 1950 a Navy vessel sprayed bacteria over San Francisco from offshore. The city was not told. The Army later said the organisms were harmless. Stanford Hospital then recorded a rare Serratia cluster, and one man died.
